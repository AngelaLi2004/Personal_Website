Spatial Inequality in Public Library Accessibility

New York City

Course: GGIS 224 – Geographic Information Science
Institution: University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign
Instructor: Dr. Wang
Author: Angela Li
Date: May 2025

Tools: R (sf, tidycensus, r5r, spdep, tmap), OpenStreetMap, ACS

Project Overview

Public libraries play a critical role in providing equitable access to information, digital resources, and community services.
This project examines whether public library accessibility in New York City is spatially equitable, and how accessibility patterns relate to race and income at the census tract level.

Using walking-based network accessibility, spatial autocorrelation analysis (LISA), and regression modeling, this study evaluates whether neighborhoods with higher proportions of racial minorities or lower income experience systematically poorer access to public libraries.

Data Sources

  • Public library locations: NYC Open Data (NYPL, BPL, QPL branches)
  • Census tracts & demographics: ACS 5-year estimates (via tidycensus)
  • Road network: OpenStreetMap PBF (processed with r5r)

All datasets were projected to EPSG: 2263 (NAD83 / New York Long Island).

Methodology

Conceptual Workflow

The workflow integrates demographic data, library locations, and walking networks to model accessibility and evaluate spatial inequality.

Accessibility Modeling

  • Census tract centroids were generated using st_centroid()
  • Walking travel time to the nearest library was computed using r5r::travel_time_matrix()
  • Median walking time (travel_time_p50) was used as the accessibility metric
  • Isochrone-based service areas were tested but excluded due to computational limits

Results

1. Spatial Distribution of Libraries

Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) shows that public libraries are highly concentrated in Manhattan and central Brooklyn, while southern Staten Island and eastern Queens exhibit sparse coverage.

2. Tract-Level Accessibility Patterns

Mapping median walking time reveals clear spatial disparities:

  • Most tracts fall within 10–15 minutes
  • Longer travel times (20–30 minutes) are concentrated in southern Staten Island, southeastern Queens, and parts of the Bronx

These patterns suggest uneven infrastructure and service distribution across the city.

3. Socioeconomic Context

To contextualize accessibility, tract-level demographic variables were mapped:

  • % Black population: higher in central Brooklyn, SE Queens, Bronx
  • % Hispanic population: higher in northern Manhattan, South Bronx
  • Median household income: highest in Manhattan core and NW Brooklyn

These spatial patterns overlap with several underserved accessibility zones.

4. Spatial Autocorrelation (LISA)

Local Moran’s I analysis reveals statistically significant clustering:

  • High–High clusters (poor access) in southern Queens, eastern Brooklyn, Staten Island
  • Low–Low clusters (good access) in Manhattan and central Brooklyn

Global Moran’s I = 0.3825, indicating moderate positive spatial autocorrelation.
This confirms that library access is not randomly distributed across NYC.

5. Regression Analysis

An Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) model was used: travel_time_p50 ~ %Black + %Hispanic + Median Income

Key findings:

  • Only % Hispanic showed a statistically significant (p < 0.05) negative association with travel time
  • % Black population and income were not significant
  • Model explanatory power was very low (R² = 0.0036)

Diagnostic plots indicate acceptable assumptions but high variability, suggesting that linear global models poorly capture accessibility inequality.

Discussion & Limitations

  • Spatial clustering (LISA) reveals inequities not captured by regression
  • Accessibility inequality likely operates locally and non-linearly
  • Key limitations:
    • No population-demand or library capacity adjustment
    • Isochrone-based service counts excluded
    • Simplified linear modeling

Conclusions

This project demonstrates that public library accessibility in NYC is spatially unequal, with underserved clusters concentrated in southern Queens, the Bronx, and Staten Island.

While regression results show limited explanatory power, spatial autocorrelation analysis reveals clear place-based inequality, highlighting the importance of spatial methods for evaluating public infrastructure equity.

Future work will incorporate:

  • Isochrone-based service areas
  • Spatial or non-linear regression models
  • Additional predictors such as transit access and population demand

Skills Demonstrated

  • Network-based accessibility modeling (r5r)
  • Spatial autocorrelation analysis (LISA, Moran’s I)
  • Census data integration (ACS, tidycensus)
  • GIS visualization and spatial reasoning
